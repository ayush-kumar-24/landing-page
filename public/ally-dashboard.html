Customers, market and positioning

Who you serve, how big that is, and why they should pick you. Most founder confusion downstream starts with vagueness here.

17
TermFull FormWhat It Is Used ForWhat It Actually Means
ICPIdeal Customer ProfileFiltering leads, targeting ad spend, writing copy, qualifying deals, deciding what to build nextThe exact type of customer you are built to win — not everyone who could buy, but the narrow slice where you win fastest, cheapest and most profitably. A sharp ICP is a filter you say NO with.
Buyer Persona—Aligning marketing, sales and product around one specific humanA fictional but evidence-based sketch of one real customer type. Powerful when built from actual interviews; useless when invented in a meeting room.
TAMTotal Addressable MarketSizing the opportunity in a pitch deck; sanity-checking ambitionEvery rupee that could theoretically be spent on your category. Largely a vanity number — nobody has ever captured their TAM.
SAMServiceable Addressable MarketRealistic market sizing for strategy and investor conversationsThe slice of TAM you can actually reach given your model, geography, language and channel.
SOMServiceable Obtainable MarketSetting believable 1–3 year revenue targetsWhat you can genuinely capture given your team, capital and competition. The only market number worth planning against.
PMFProduct–Market FitDeciding when to stop iterating and start pouring fuel on growthThe point where customers pull the product out of your hands instead of you pushing it at them. If you are asking whether you have it, you do not.
JTBDJobs To Be DoneProduct prioritisation, messaging, understanding why people switchPeople do not buy your product; they hire it to make progress in their life. Understand the job, not the demographic.
USPUnique Selling PropositionAds, packaging, pitch decks, the one-line answer to 'why you?'The one thing you do that competitors cannot easily copy or credibly claim.
UVPUnique Value PropositionHomepage headline, sales deck opener, cold email first lineA plain sentence stating who it is for, what it does, and why it is better. If it takes a paragraph, you do not have one yet.
Positioning—Brand strategy, pricing power, defending against competitorsThe space you occupy in the customer's head relative to alternatives. You do not choose it — you earn it, and the market confirms it.
Segmentation—Serving different customer groups with different offers and pricingCutting your market into groups that behave differently enough to deserve different treatment. If the segments behave the same, they are not segments.
Beachhead Market—Choosing where to launch first when you cannot afford to be everywhereThe smallest market you can completely dominate before expanding. Win small deliberately so you can win big credibly.
VoCVoice of CustomerRoadmap decisions, copywriting, reducing churnStructured listening to what customers literally say, in their own words — not your polished summary of it.
NPSNet Promoter ScoreTracking loyalty and word-of-mouth potential over timeA 0–10 'would you recommend us' score, expressed as promoters minus detractors. Directionally useful, easily gamed. Watch the trend, not the number.
CSATCustomer Satisfaction ScoreMeasuring one specific interaction — a delivery, a support ticket, an onboarding callHow happy someone was with a single touchpoint. Tactical feedback, not a strategic signal.
Moat—Defensibility conversations with investors, and with yourselfWhatever stops a better-funded competitor from copying you in six months — network effects, switching costs, brand, data, regulation, or unfair distribution.
Category Creation—Long-horizon brand strategy when you refuse to be comparedInventing a new market instead of competing inside an existing one. Expensive, slow, and occasionally worth everything.

Product, build and delivery

How an idea becomes a shipped, working thing — and the vocabulary your engineers and designers already use.

21
TermFull FormWhat It Is Used ForWhat It Actually Means
MVPMinimum Viable ProductTesting your riskiest assumption with the least possible spendThe smallest thing you can build that still teaches you whether people want it. It is not a cheap version of the full product — it is an experiment with a user interface.
MLPMinimum Lovable ProductLaunching into a crowded category where 'viable' is not enoughAn MVP people actually enjoy using. In a saturated market, merely viable is invisible.
POCProof of ConceptAnswering 'is this even technically possible?' before committing budgetA deliberately throwaway build. Never ship a POC — it was not made to survive contact with real users.
Pilot—De-risking a full rollout by running it small and real firstA time-boxed live deployment with one or a few real customers, usually paid. The bridge between demo and contract.
Roadmap—Aligning team, investors and clients on sequence and intentAn ordered set of bets, not a delivery promise. Anything more than a quarter out is a hypothesis wearing a date.
Backlog—Holding everything you are consciously not doing right nowThe list of requested-but-not-committed work. If it only ever grows, you are collecting, not prioritising.
Sprint—Giving the team a repeatable rhythm and a fixed finish lineA short fixed window — usually one or two weeks — in which scope is frozen and something ships.
User Story—Writing requirements an engineer can actually build from'As a [user], I want [action], so that [outcome].' Forces you to state the why, not just the what.
Acceptance Criteria—Ending arguments about whether something is finishedThe specific, testable conditions that must be true for work to count as done. Write them before the work starts.
Wireframe—Agreeing structure before spending money on visual designA grey-box layout showing where things go, deliberately before how they look.
Prototype—Testing a flow with real users before a line of code is writtenA clickable fake. Cheap to throw away, which is the entire point.
Design System—Keeping a product visually coherent as more people touch itA reusable kit of components, colours, type and rules so nobody reinvents the button on every screen.
Tech DebtTechnical DebtDeciding when to slow down and clean up versus keep shippingShortcuts taken to move faster now, on which you pay interest later. Some debt is a smart trade; unmanaged debt compounds until velocity dies.
Feature Creep—Diagnosing why launches keep slipping and the product feels bloatedThe product quietly growing features nobody validated, usually because saying no felt impolite.
Scope Creep—Protecting margin and timelines on client or agency workThe agreed deliverable expanding while the price and deadline stay exactly where they were.
QAQuality AssuranceCatching defects before your customer doesSystematic testing before release. The cost of skipping it is always paid twice — once in support, once in reputation.
UATUser Acceptance TestingFinal sign-off before go-liveThe actual end user confirms it solves their problem — a different question from whether the code runs.
Beta—Controlled exposure to real users before public launchA near-finished release given to a limited group who have been told things may break.
GAGeneral AvailabilityMarking a product as fully, publicly launchedOpen to everyone, fully supported, with no 'we're still testing' excuse available.
Iteration—Improving something in deliberate cycles rather than one big betOne loop of build → measure → learn. Speed of iteration beats the quality of any single decision.
APIApplication Programming InterfaceLetting your product talk to other software, and partners plug into youA defined doorway through which one system requests something from another. It is how integrations, partnerships and platforms happen.
